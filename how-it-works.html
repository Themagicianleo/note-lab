Note Lab

How it works

A beginner's guide to sound, notes and what Note Lab measures.

1. Sound and frequency

Sound is air vibrating: pushed back and forth very quickly, like a guitar string or your vocal cords moving. Frequency is how many times per second that vibration repeats. It's measured in hertz (Hz): 440 Hz means 440 vibrations every second.

Humans hear roughly from 20 Hz (a very deep rumble) to 20 000 Hz (a thin, high hiss), and less of the top end as we get older.

2. Pitch

Pitch is how high or low a sound feels to us. Faster vibration = higher pitch. For a single musical note, the pitch you hear matches its lowest repeating frequency, the fundamental. Not every sound has a pitch: a whisper, the "sss" in "sun" or wind noise don't repeat regularly, so there's no note to find. That's when Note Lab says "no clear pitch".

3. Notes, semitones and octaves

Western music uses 12 note names that repeat: C, C#, D, D#, E, F, F#, G, G#, A, A#, B. Each step is a semitone (one piano key, white or black). C# ("C sharp") is the black key just above C. It can also be called D♭ ("D flat").

An octave is 12 semitones, and it doubles the frequency. Notes an octave apart sound "the same, but higher", so they get the same name. The number after the name says which octave: A4 is the A above middle C, A3 is one octave lower.

4. Cents: how in tune you are

A cent is one hundredth of a semitone. "+12 cents" means a little sharp (too high); "−20 cents" means a little flat (too low). ±50 cents is the furthest you can be from a note before you're closer to the next one. Many people start to notice a difference of roughly 5–25 cents, depending on the sound and on practice.

5. The formula (and a worked example)

Note Lab uses the standard "equal temperament" formula, with A4 = 440 Hz as the reference (you can change it in Settings):

note number n = 69 + 12 × log2(frequency ÷ 440)
nearest note m = round(n)
cents          = 100 × (n − m)
frequency      = 440 × 2^((m − 69) ÷ 12)

"Note number" counts piano keys: A4 = 69, middle C = 60. log2 means "how many times do I double 1 to get this number".

Example: what note is 330 Hz?

  1. 330 ÷ 440 = 0.75
  2. log2(0.75) = −0.41504 (going down from 1 to 0.75 is about 0.42 of a "halving")
  3. × 12 = −4.98045 semitones below A4
  4. n = 69 − 4.98045 = 64.01955
  5. Nearest note: 64. That's 5 semitones below A4, which is E4
  6. Cents: 100 × 0.01955 = +2 cents (very slightly sharp)

So Note Lab shows E4 · 330.0 Hz · +2 cents. A perfectly tuned E4 would be 440 × 2^(−5/12) = 329.63 Hz.

6. Harmonics: why one note is many frequencies

A real note isn't one pure frequency. Alongside the fundamental there are quieter harmonics at 2×, 3×, 4×… that frequency. For A3 (220 Hz): 440, 660, 880 Hz and so on. How loud each harmonic is gives an instrument or voice its tone colour (timbre). That's why a piano and a flute playing the same note sound different.

You can see harmonics in the Spectrum tab. Surprisingly, the loudest peak is often a harmonic, not the note you hear. Vowels boost certain regions (called formants), so a low "ah" can be loudest around 700 Hz. That's why Note Lab doesn't just pick the loudest frequency.

7. How Note Lab finds the pitch

About 60 times a second, Note Lab takes the latest ~0.04 seconds of sound from the microphone and uses a method called YIN. In plain words: a pitched sound repeats itself, so YIN slides a copy of the sound along the original and looks for the shortest shift where the two match almost perfectly. That shift is one vibration, and frequency = samples per second ÷ samples per vibration.

  • Volume threshold: very quiet sound is ignored, so background hiss doesn't make random notes.
  • Clarity check: if the sound doesn't repeat clearly enough (breath, consonants, noise), it says "no clear pitch" instead of guessing.
  • Range: in Voice mode it only looks between the lowest and highest pitch of your chosen voice range, which rules out impossible answers such as an octave too low.
  • Smoothing: it shows the middle value (median) of the last few readings so the display doesn't flicker. More smoothing = steadier but slower.

Measured accuracy and known weak spots are listed in the project README. Most measurements so far use computer-generated test sounds, which are easier than real voices.

8. The two kinds of "dB"

Decibels (dB) compare loudness on a scale where every 20 dB is 10× in amplitude. Note Lab shows dBFS, "decibels relative to full scale": 0 dBFS is the loudest sound the microphone input can handle, and everything else is negative (−20, −40, …).

This is not the "dB" of a sound level meter (like "a conversation is about 60 dB"). That needs a calibrated microphone. A phone's microphone isn't calibrated, and the phone may adjust its own sensitivity.

9. What Note Lab can't do (yet)

  • Chords: it follows one note at a time. With several notes at once, the result can't be trusted.
  • Octave mistakes still happen sometimes, especially with rough or very breathy voices.
  • Very low notes (below about 50 Hz) need the tuner's "Low" range, which reacts a little slower.
  • Noisy rooms make "no clear pitch" more common.

10. Privacy

All sound is processed on your device, inside the browser. Microphone audio is never stored on your device, uploaded or sent anywhere. The one exception you control: Record on the Voice tab keeps a recording in this page's memory so you can listen back. It is never saved or uploaded, and it is gone when you tap Delete, stop the microphone or close the page. An audio file you choose to analyse is read in the page too, never uploaded. Only your settings and training scores are saved, in this browser, so they're there next time.

Quick glossary

Hz (hertz)
vibrations per second
Fundamental
the lowest repeating frequency of a note; sets its pitch
Harmonic
a quieter frequency at a whole-number multiple of the fundamental
Semitone
one piano key; 1/12 of an octave
Octave
double the frequency; same note name
Cent
1/100 of a semitone
Interval
the distance between two notes, e.g. a perfect 5th = 7 semitones
Sharp / flat
a bit too high / a bit too low (or the black-key names, C# / D♭)
dBFS
level compared with the loudest the input can handle (0 dBFS)
EQ
equaliser: turning ranges of frequencies up or down
