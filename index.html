What am I looking at?

Every sound is a mix of many frequencies. This chart shows how loud each frequency is: low (deep) sounds on the left, high (bright) sounds on the right. Every doubling of frequency (100 → 200 Hz, 1k → 2k) is one octave and takes the same width, which matches how we hear. Height is the level in dBFS: higher = louder.

An equaliser (EQ) turns some of these ranges up or down. The shaded bands are the names people use when talking about EQ (the edges are conventions, not exact):

  • Sub-bass (20–60 Hz): felt more than heard: deep rumble, the thump of a kick drum.
  • Bass (60–250 Hz): warmth and fullness; bass notes, low piano, men's voices.
  • Low mids (250–500 Hz): body; too much sounds muddy or boxy.
  • Mids (500 Hz–2 kHz): where most instruments and vowel sounds live.
  • Upper mids (2–4 kHz): clarity and bite; our ears are most sensitive here.
  • Presence (4–6 kHz): closeness and definition; too much sounds harsh.
  • Brilliance (6–20 kHz): air and sparkle; cymbals and the hiss of "s".

Try this: hum (bass and low mids), say "sssss" (brilliance), say "shhh" (a bit lower than "s"), sing "ee" then "oo" on the same note and watch the peaks move, or play one note and spot its harmonics: peaks at 1×, 2×, 3×… the note's frequency.

The loudest peak isn't always the note you hear. A voice's loudest frequency is often a harmonic boosted by the vowel shape, so the Tuner and Voice modes use a pitch detector instead.

    Measured

    What am I looking at?

    Sound is tiny, fast changes in air pressure. The microphone turns them into a voltage, and this line shows that signal over time: up = pressure a little above normal, down = a little below. Left to right is time; the labels show milliseconds (ms, thousandths of a second).

    Try: whistle or hum a steady note and you'll see a shape that repeats. One repeat is a period (T). Higher notes have shorter periods, so more repeats fit on screen. Louder sounds make a taller wave. A pure tone looks like a smooth sine curve; a voice or instrument has a more complicated shape because it contains harmonics (see the Harmonics tool).

    Zoom: "time zoom" changes how many milliseconds fit across the screen. "Height zoom" only magnifies the picture, not the sound. Steady display starts each picture where the wave crosses the centre line going up, which is what real oscilloscopes call triggering.

    Measured, but relative: the height is a fraction of the largest signal the input can take ("full scale"), not a pressure in pascals, because phone and laptop mics aren't calibrated. A wave that flattens at the top and bottom is clipping: too loud for the input.

    Formula: frequency from the period 
    f = 1 / T
    • f: frequency, in hertz (Hz) = cycles per second
    • T: period, the time for one cycle, in seconds (s)

    Worked example: T = 2.2727 ms = 0.0022727 s, so f = 1 / 0.0022727 ≈ 440 Hz (A4).

    Source: the definition of frequency (any physics textbook, e.g. the waves chapter of Halliday, Resnick & Walker, Fundamentals of Physics). The period shown is measured by the app's YIN pitch detector, and f is then calculated as 1/T.

    Reference pitch (A4)

    440 Hz is the modern standard. Some orchestras tune a little higher (441–443), "baroque pitch" is about 415, and some people prefer 432. Every note name, cents value and training tone follows this setting.

    Tuner volume threshold: −60 dBFS

    This is the tuner's sensitivity. Left (lower number) = more sensitive, picks up quiet notes but also background noise. Right = only louder sounds count. Watch the input level while you're silent and set this above it.

    Voice detection range

    The detector only looks for pitches inside this range. That rules out answers that are impossible for your voice, like a reading an octave too low. If a note you sing is outside the range, you'll see "no clear pitch", so choose a range that includes all your notes.

    Voice & sing-back volume threshold: −55 dBFS

    Sounds quieter than this are ignored, so breathing and background noise don't produce random notes. In a noisy room, move it right (to a higher number), above the level the meter shows when you're silent.

    Voice smoothing: Balanced
    FastSteady

    Steady: the needle and line wobble less, but take longer to follow a new note and can hide short gaps. Fast: shows every change straight away, including vibrato and the odd wrong reading.

    Input level
    –

    dBFS = decibels below the loudest sound the microphone input can take (0 dBFS). It shows how loud the sound is to this microphone. It is not a calibrated sound level meter. The thin line marks the volume threshold: anything quieter is ignored.

    Good to know

    • Works best with one note at a time. Chords are not supported.
    • "No clear note" or "no clear pitch" means the sound was too quiet or not clearly pitched (breath, a consonant, noise), so nothing is shown rather than a guess.
    • Your range-finder results stay in this page only and disappear when you close it. Settings and training scores are saved on this device only.
