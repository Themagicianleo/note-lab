What am I looking at?

Every sound is a mix of many frequencies. This chart shows how loud each frequency is: low (deep) sounds on the left, high (bright) sounds on the right. Every doubling of frequency (100 → 200 Hz, 1k → 2k) is one octave and takes the same width, which matches how we hear. Height is the level in dBFS: higher = louder.

An equaliser (EQ) turns some of these ranges up or down. The shaded bands are the names people use when talking about EQ (the edges are conventions, not exact):

  • Sub-bass (20–60 Hz): felt more than heard: deep rumble, the thump of a kick drum.
  • Bass (60–250 Hz): warmth and fullness; bass notes, low piano, men's voices.
  • Low mids (250–500 Hz): body; too much sounds muddy or boxy.
  • Mids (500 Hz–2 kHz): where most instruments and vowel sounds live.
  • Upper mids (2–4 kHz): clarity and bite; our ears are most sensitive here.
  • Presence (4–6 kHz): closeness and definition; too much sounds harsh.
  • Brilliance (6–20 kHz): air and sparkle; cymbals and the hiss of "s".

Try this: hum (bass and low mids), say "sssss" (brilliance), say "shhh" (a bit lower than "s"), sing "ee" then "oo" on the same note and watch the peaks move, or play one note and spot its harmonics: peaks at 1×, 2×, 3×… the note's frequency.

The loudest peak isn't always the note you hear. A voice's loudest frequency is often a harmonic boosted by the vowel shape, so the Tuner and Voice modes use a pitch detector instead.

This shows what your microphone hears. Phone and laptop mics pick up very low (below ~100 Hz) and very high sounds less well, so those parts may look quieter than they really are.

    Measured

    What am I looking at?

    Sound is tiny, fast changes in air pressure. The microphone turns them into a voltage, and this line shows that signal over time: up = pressure a little above normal, down = a little below. Left to right is time; the labels show milliseconds (ms, thousandths of a second).

    Try: whistle or hum a steady note and you'll see a shape that repeats. One repeat is a period (T). Higher notes have shorter periods, so more repeats fit on screen. Louder sounds make a taller wave. A pure tone looks like a smooth sine curve; a voice or instrument has a more complicated shape because it contains harmonics (see the Harmonics tool).

    Zoom: "time zoom" changes how many milliseconds fit across the screen. "Height zoom" only magnifies the picture, not the sound. Steady display starts each picture where the wave crosses the centre line going up, which is what real oscilloscopes call triggering.

    Measured, but relative: the height is a fraction of the largest signal the input can take ("full scale"), not a pressure in pascals, because phone and laptop mics aren't calibrated. A wave that flattens at the top and bottom is clipping: too loud for the input.

    Formula: frequency from the period 
    f = 1 / T
    • f: frequency, in hertz (Hz) = cycles per second
    • T: period, the time for one cycle, in seconds (s)

    Worked example: T = 2.2727 ms = 0.0022727 s, so f = 1 / 0.0022727 ≈ 440 Hz (A4).

    Source: the definition of frequency (any physics textbook, e.g. the waves chapter of Halliday, Resnick & Walker, Fundamentals of Physics). The period shown is measured by the app's YIN pitch detector, and f is then calculated as 1/T.

    Measured

    Bars: dB relative to the loudest harmonic (0 dB). Live A B

    Compare two sounds

    What am I looking at?

    A musical note isn't one frequency. It's a fundamental (f₀, the pitch you hear) plus harmonics at 2×, 3×, 4×… that frequency. Bar 1 is the fundamental, bar 2 the 2nd harmonic, and so on. Taller = louder.

    The pattern of bars is what makes a flute, a violin and your voice sound different on the same note (their timbre). Try singing "ee" and then "oo" on one note: the pitch stays, but the bar pattern changes, because your mouth shape boosts different harmonics.

    Compare: capture your voice as A, then capture another sound as B, or pick a calculated tone for B. The calculated tones come from maths (their Fourier series), not from the microphone. Play it and capture it with the mic as A to see how your speaker and microphone change a "perfect" tone; that's measured vs model.

    Cents off shows how far each harmonic is from exactly n × f₀. Most sounds are close to 0; stiff strings like a piano's have higher harmonics a little sharp.

    Relative, not absolute: levels are compared with the loudest harmonic of the same sound, so a quiet and a loud recording of the same sound give the same bars. Phone and laptop mics pick up low and very high frequencies less well, which changes the bars a little.

    Formulas: harmonic series and relative level 
    f_n = n × f₀        (n = 1, 2, 3, …)
L = 20 × log10(a / a_ref)   dB
    • f_n: frequency of harmonic n, in Hz; f₀: fundamental frequency, in Hz; n: harmonic number (no unit)
    • L: level difference in decibels (dB); a, a_ref: two amplitudes in the same unit (here: of a harmonic, and of the loudest harmonic)

    Worked example: f₀ = 220 Hz gives harmonics at 440, 660, 880 Hz. If the 2nd harmonic has half the amplitude of the loudest, L = 20 × log10(0.5) = −6.02 dB.

    Calculated tones (Fourier series): square wave: odd harmonics only, amplitude 1/n (3rd: −9.54 dB, 5th: −13.98 dB). Sawtooth: all harmonics, amplitude 1/n (2nd: −6.02 dB).

    Sources: Fourier's theorem (any periodic signal is a sum of sines at whole-number multiples of its fundamental), and the standard Fourier series of square and sawtooth waves, e.g. in any signals or acoustics textbook such as Kinsler et al., Fundamentals of Acoustics. Decibels for amplitudes use 20 × log10.

    Calculated · played as tones

    Blue: the two tones added together (calculated). Orange dashes: the envelope ±2·|cos(π·Δf·t)|. The moving line shows where you are while it plays.

    What am I looking at?

    When two tones are close in frequency, their waves keep drifting in and out of step. When the peaks line up, they add up and the sound gets louder; half a beat later, a peak meets a trough, they cancel and it gets quieter. That slow swelling and fading is called beats.

    It happens |f₁ − f₂| times per second. Slide Δ towards 0 and the beats slow down; at 0 they stop. That's how musicians tune: they play two notes and adjust until the beats disappear. Above roughly 15–20 Hz (this varies with the listener and the frequency) the pulses are too fast to count and the sound turns rough; further apart, you hear two separate notes.

    One tone per ear: with headphones, each ear gets only one tone, so the waves never add up in the air. Many people still hear a faint pulsing ("binaural beats"), which is created by the brain. It's weaker, and it only works for fairly small differences.

    The graph is calculated from the formula, not recorded. Your speakers and room may add their own colouring to what you hear.

    Formulas: beat frequency 
    sin(2πf₁t) + sin(2πf₂t) = 2·cos(π(f₁−f₂)t) · sin(π(f₁+f₂)t)
f_beat = |f₁ − f₂|        T_beat = 1 / f_beat
    • f₁, f₂: the two tones' frequencies, in Hz; t: time, in s
    • f_beat: beats per second, in Hz; T_beat: time from one loud moment to the next, in s
    • The heard pitch is about the average, (f₁ + f₂) / 2

    Worked example: f₁ = 440 Hz, f₂ = 443 Hz → f_beat = |440 − 443| = 3 Hz, so a beat every 1/3 ≈ 0.333 s, heard at 441.5 Hz.

    Why |cos|: the loudness follows the size of 2·cos(π(f₁−f₂)t), which reaches its biggest size twice per cycle of the cosine, i.e. |f₁ − f₂| times a second.

    Sources: the trigonometric sum-to-product identity; beats are covered in e.g. Rossing, Moore & Wheeler, The Science of Sound. The "roughness above ~15–20 Hz" remark is an approximate description of hearing research (e.g. Plomp & Levelt, 1965), not an exact formula.

    What am I looking at?

    A spectrogram is a picture of sound over time. Across is time (newest on the right), up is frequency (low at the bottom), and colour is loudness: dark = quiet, bright yellow = loud.

    Try: sing a slow scale and watch a staircase of lines. The bottom line is your fundamental, and the lines above it are its harmonics, at 2×, 3×… Whistle a slide and you'll draw a curve. Say "sss" and you'll see a bright smear high up, with no lines: noise has no harmonics. Clap: a thin vertical stripe, because a short click contains many frequencies at once.

    The trade-off (move the window slider): each column is an FFT of the last N samples. A long window (8192) separates frequencies finely (Δf ≈ 6 Hz) but blurs time: a clap becomes a wide smear. A short window (512) shows timing sharply (≈ 11 ms) but frequencies become thick blurry bands (Δf ≈ 94 Hz). You can't have both at once: Δt × Δf = 1 always.

    Measured, but relative: colours show dBFS (relative to the input's maximum), not true sound pressure. Phone mics pick up very low and very high frequencies less well, so those regions may look darker than the sound really is.

    Formulas: time vs frequency resolution 
    Δt = N / fs        Δf = fs / N        Δt × Δf = 1
    • N: window size, in samples (no unit)
    • fs: sample rate, in samples per second (Hz), e.g. 48 000
    • Δt: how long each column's window lasts, in s; Δf: spacing between the FFT's frequency bins, in Hz

    Worked example: N = 2048, fs = 48 000 Hz → Δt = 2048 / 48 000 = 0.0427 s (42.7 ms), Δf = 48 000 / 2048 = 23.4 Hz, and 0.0427 × 23.4 = 1.

    Good to know: with the Hann window this app uses, two tones need to be about 2 bins apart (≈ 2·Δf) to show as separate lines. New columns arrive about every 17 ms even when the window is longer, so neighbouring columns share most of their sound (they overlap); that's why long windows look smeared sideways.

    Sources: the discrete Fourier transform (bin spacing fs/N); the short-time Fourier transform and its time–frequency trade-off (Gabor, 1946), e.g. in Smith, Spectral Audio Signal Processing, or Oppenheim & Schafer, Discrete-Time Signal Processing.

    Reference pitch (A4)

    440 Hz is the modern standard. Some orchestras tune a little higher (441–443), "baroque pitch" is about 415, and some people prefer 432. Every note name, cents value and training tone follows this setting.

    Tuner volume threshold: −60 dBFS

    This is the tuner's sensitivity. Left (lower number) = more sensitive, picks up quiet notes but also background noise. Right = only louder sounds count. Watch the input level while you're silent and set this above it.

    Voice detection range

    The detector only looks for pitches inside this range. That rules out answers that are impossible for your voice, like a reading an octave too low. If a note you sing is outside the range, you'll see "no clear pitch", so choose a range that includes all your notes.

    Voice & sing-back volume threshold: −55 dBFS

    Sounds quieter than this are ignored, so breathing and background noise don't produce random notes. In a noisy room, move it right (to a higher number), above the level the meter shows when you're silent.

    Voice smoothing: Balanced
    FastSteady

    Steady: the needle and line wobble less, but take longer to follow a new note and can hide short gaps. Fast: shows every change straight away, including vibrato and the odd wrong reading.

    Good to know

    • Works best with one note at a time. Chords are not supported.
    • "No clear note" or "no clear pitch" means the sound was too quiet or not clearly pitched (breath, a consonant, noise), so nothing is shown rather than a guess.
    • Your range-finder results stay in this page only and disappear when you close it. Settings and training scores are saved on this device only.
